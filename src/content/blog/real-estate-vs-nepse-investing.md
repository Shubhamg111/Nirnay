Ask an older generation in Nepal how to build wealth, and land or property usually
comes up first. Ask someone newer to investing, and NEPSE often comes up instead.
Both are legitimate paths — but they behave very differently.

## The basic trade-offs

| | Real estate | NEPSE |
|---|---|---|
| Minimum capital needed | High | Low — you can start small |
| Liquidity | Low — can take months to sell | High — can sell in a trading session |
| Ongoing effort | Property management, legal paperwork | Minimal once invested |
| Price transparency | Often unclear, negotiation-based | Public, updated live during market hours |
| Volatility | Generally slower-moving | Can swing significantly, faster |
| Entry costs | Registration fees, taxes, broker commissions | Lower — brokerage commission only |

## Why real estate has been a traditional favorite

- Feels tangible — you can see and use what you own
- Historically, land in growing areas like the Kathmandu Valley has appreciated
  significantly over long periods
- Doesn't require watching daily price movements
- Can double as a place to live while it appreciates

## Where real estate gets harder

- Needs a large amount of capital upfront, which limits how early you can start
- Illiquid — if you need cash quickly, selling property isn't fast
- Legal and paperwork complexity (land registration, ownership verification)
- Property prices in Nepal aren't always transparent or easy to verify against a
  public benchmark

## Why NEPSE appeals to newer investors

- You can start with a relatively small amount and add to it over time
- Highly liquid — you can convert holdings to cash within a trading session
- Transparent, publicly available pricing
- Easier to diversify — you can hold small positions across many companies instead
  of concentrating your entire net worth in one property

## Where NEPSE gets harder

- Prices can be volatile, and it takes discipline not to panic during downturns
- Requires at least some ongoing attention, especially if picking individual stocks
- Historically has had periods of extended stagnation, not just growth

## A reasonable way to think about it

These aren't mutually exclusive. A common long-term approach is to build a NEPSE
and mutual fund portfolio first — since it needs less capital to start — and
consider real estate later once you've accumulated enough savings to make a
property purchase without it being your only asset. That way, a downturn in one
doesn't wipe out your entire net worth.
