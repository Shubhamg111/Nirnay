Once your Demat and CDS account is open, the next question is usually: do you buy individual shares on NEPSE, or put your money into a mutual fund instead? Both are valid starting points — the right one depends on how much time and attention you can realistically give it, and on understanding a few mechanics that trip up a lot of first-time investors in Nepal specifically.

## The core difference

**Buying individual NEPSE stocks** means you choose which companies to invest in — a specific bank, hydropower company, or insurer — and you're responsible for researching them, tracking their performance, and deciding when to buy or sell.

**Mutual funds** pool money from many investors and are managed by a professional fund manager who picks the mix of stocks for you. You buy units of the fund, not shares of individual companies.

## What the data actually shows

This isn't just a theoretical trade-off. <cite index="68-1">A comparison of the Mutual Fund Index against the NEPSE Index from mid-2020 to early 2025 found NEPSE delivered a slightly higher total return, 90.86% versus 84.56% for the mutual fund index</cite> — but <cite index="68-1">the mutual fund index was noticeably less volatile, and its maximum drawdown was 29.12% compared to NEPSE's 43.26%</cite>. In plain terms: individual stocks had a bit more upside over that period, but mutual funds fell a lot less during the bad stretches. That's the trade-off in a nutshell, and it held up in the actual data, not just in theory.

It's also worth knowing the market you're entering isn't calm right now. <cite index="75-1">The number of online trading accounts in Nepal grew from about 7.4 lakh in 2020 to over 63 lakh in 2026</cite>, and <cite index="71-1">NEPSE has a history of sharp drawdowns — a 30–50% drop from peak to trough, including a 2021–2023 stretch that erased significant wealth for people who bought at the top without understanding valuations</cite>. Whichever path you choose, go in expecting volatility, not a steady climb.

## Compare the basics

| | Individual stocks | Mutual funds |
|---|---|---|
| Research needed | High — you pick each company | Low — manager decides |
| Diversification | Depends on you | Built in, spread across many companies |
| Time commitment | Ongoing | Minimal after initial choice |
| Control | Full control over each holding | Limited — you trust the manager |
| Typical volatility | Can be high, stock-specific | Usually smoother, spread across holdings |
| Fees | Broker commission per trade | Fund expense ratio (ongoing) + broker commission if closed-end |

## When individual stocks make more sense

- You're willing to spend real time reading company reports, following sector news, and tracking NEPSE announcements
- You want full control over exactly what you own
- You're comfortable with a single company's bad quarter affecting your portfolio more directly
- You understand that <cite index="71-1">banking stocks dominate NEPSE, so a regulatory change or credit crisis in the banking sector can drag down the whole index</cite> even if you've diversified across a handful of companies

## When mutual funds make more sense

- You want exposure to NEPSE without picking individual winners yourself
- You'd rather check your investment occasionally than track it daily
- You're just starting out and want to reduce the risk of concentrating your money in one or two companies you don't fully understand yet

## The part most beginners get wrong: closed-end vs. open-end funds

This is the single biggest source of confusion for new mutual fund investors in Nepal, and it's worth getting right before you put money in.

**Closed-end funds** are what most people encounter first, because they're the ones marketed through IPO-style subscriptions. <cite index="83-1">Nearly every fund a retail investor can easily buy in Nepal is a closed-end fund.</cite> <cite index="77-1">You can buy in at the initial offering at the par value of NPR 10, the same way you'd apply for a share IPO — or afterward on the NEPSE secondary market through your broker and Demat account, at whatever price the market sets that day.</cite> Here's the part that surprises people: <cite index="83-1">closed-end units in Nepal almost always trade below their Net Asset Value (NAV) once listed</cite>. So checking your app and seeing your fund's market price sitting under its reported NAV isn't a sign something went wrong — it's normal for this fund structure, and it means buying at the par-value IPO is often not the advantageous entry it looks like, since the same units frequently trade cheaper on the open market later.

**Open-end funds** work differently. <cite index="83-1">They have no fixed size and no maturity date — you buy units directly from the fund manager at NAV, and you redeem them back to the manager at NAV</cite>, so there's no separate market price to worry about and no discount trap. <cite index="71-1">This is typically the safer, more straightforward structure for beginners</cite>, but <cite index="77-1">these funds don't list on NEPSE — you buy through the fund manager's office, designated bank branches, an authorised agent, an online portal, or a recurring SIP instruction instead of through your regular broker.</cite> <cite index="83-1">They're also still a small minority of what's available</cite> compared to the closed-end funds that dominate the market.

Before you buy any fund, ask directly: is this open-end or closed-end? It changes how you buy it, how (and whether) you can exit early, and whether the price you see is the price you'll actually get.

## What it costs to actually trade

For individual stocks bought and sold through your broker, <cite index="81-1">SEBON-regulated broker commission is tiered: 0.40% on trades up to NPR 50,000, stepping down to 0.37%, 0.34%, 0.30%, and finally 0.27% on trades above NPR 1 crore</cite>, charged on both the buy and the sell side. On top of that, <cite index="84-1">there's a small SEBON regulatory fee and a roughly NPR 25 DP charge per transaction</cite>. <cite index="80-1">Rates for mutual fund units, bonds, and debentures are generally lower than the standard equity rate.</cite>

Capital gains tax applies only when you sell at a profit. <cite index="84-1">Under the rates in force from mid-July 2026, a resident individual pays 7.5% on gains from shares held more than a year, and 10% if held a year or less</cite> — <cite index="84-1">this is deducted automatically at settlement, so you don't file it separately</cite>. Two details that catch people off guard: <cite index="84-1">the gain is calculated on the weighted average cost of all your units in that scrip, and bonus shares are valued at their NPR 100 par value for cost-basis purposes rather than market price</cite>, which can mean a bigger taxable gain than you'd expect on shares that came from a bonus issue.

## A common middle path

Many beginners in Nepal do both — a core position in one or two mutual funds for diversification and lower effort, plus a smaller amount in individual stocks they've researched themselves, mainly as a way to learn how the market actually behaves.

## What to check before choosing a mutual fund

- **Historical performance over several years**, not just the last few months, and ideally benchmarked against the NEPSE index itself over the same period
- **Whether it's open-end or closed-end** — see above — since this single fact determines how you'll actually buy, hold, and exit
- **The fund manager and the company running it.** <cite index="71-1">Fund management companies in Nepal include Nabil Investment Banking, NMB Capital, Global IME Capital, Citizen Investment Trust, and NIBL Capital</cite>, among others
- **The expense ratio** — what percentage of your investment goes to fees each year — and how it compares to similar funds
- **If it's closed-end and already listed**, what its current market price looks like relative to its published NAV, since buying at a steep discount is very different from buying near or above NAV

## The bigger picture

<cite index="77-1">The mutual fund industry in Nepal has grown roughly fivefold in under seven years, from about NPR 13.6 billion in assets under management in 2019 to somewhere in the NPR 61–73 billion range by 2026</cite> — so this is a genuinely growing part of the market, not a niche product. That growth doesn't mean every fund is a good one, but it does mean there are more real options than there were even a few years ago.

## Frequently Asked Questions

<details>
<summary><strong>Is NEPSE too volatile for a beginner to invest in directly?</strong></summary>

It can be. NEPSE has seen drops of 30–50% from peak to trough historically, including a multi-year bear market after the 2021 peak that hurt investors who bought in without understanding valuations. That doesn't mean beginners should avoid stocks entirely, but it does mean starting with money you can afford to see drop temporarily, and possibly starting with mutual funds first to get comfortable with how the market moves.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Why is my mutual fund's market price lower than its NAV?</strong></summary>

If it's a closed-end fund, this is normal, not a sign of a problem. Closed-end fund units in Nepal almost always trade below their Net Asset Value once listed on NEPSE, because the market price is set by buyers and sellers, not by the fund's underlying value directly. It's one of the most common points of confusion for new fund investors here.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Should I buy a mutual fund at its IPO/NFO, or wait and buy it later on NEPSE?</strong></summary>

It depends, but many funds trade below their par value after listing, which means the IPO price isn't always the cheapest entry point. If you can, compare the current secondary-market price to the fund's NAV before deciding when to buy, rather than assuming the initial offering is automatically the best deal.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>How much do I actually pay in fees and tax when I sell shares at a profit?</strong></summary>

You'll pay a tiered broker commission (roughly 0.27%–0.40% depending on trade size) on both the buy and sell, a small SEBON regulatory fee, a DP charge of around NPR 25 per transaction, and capital gains tax on any profit — 7.5% if you held the shares more than a year, 10% if a year or less. The tax is deducted automatically at settlement.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Can I do a monthly SIP into a mutual fund in Nepal like index investors do elsewhere?</strong></summary>

Only with open-end funds, which allow recurring investment instructions directly through the fund manager. Closed-end funds — which make up most of what's available in Nepal — don't work this way; you either subscribe once at the offering or buy units on the NEPSE secondary market through a broker.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Do I need a lot of money to start investing in NEPSE or a mutual fund?</strong></summary>

No. Individual shares and mutual fund units can both be bought in relatively small amounts once your Demat/CDS account is set up. The bigger initial cost is usually the account setup itself, not a high minimum investment — though very small trades pay a higher percentage in broker commission, since the fee structure is tiered.

</details>
